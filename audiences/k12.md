# High school workbench guide

Status: Proposed high-school adaptation — awaiting teacher trials and classroom evidence. Younger-grade adaptations remain pending.

Start with taught concepts, teacher modeling, and a bounded choice students can explain.

## Readiness and responsibility

For the science example, check that students can read temperatures, calculate a difference, and distinguish a changed variable from a controlled one. Model any missing concept with a simple comparison before asking for critique. Offer spoken, written, visual, or other accessible ways to explain. For a different subject, agree its prerequisites and response options with the teacher first.

Students own an evidence-based explanation within a teacher-supplied task. Teachers and schools retain tool access, privacy, safeguarding, assessment, and instructional decisions. Student AI accounts are not assumed; teacher-mediated use is sufficient.

## Worked teaching example

### Fictional shade and temperature comparison

This is an authored, fictional design example, not a classroom result or a captured model response. Offer it as an optional starting point; the educator may supply a different task.

A fictional observation finds sunlit asphalt at 36°C and shaded grass at 28°C. The proposed AI contribution says shade always lowers temperature by 8°C. Both shade and surface changed; this comparison cannot isolate the effect of shade or establish an always claim.

### Interactive sequence

1. Before showing the contribution, ask what changed between the two measurements and what the comparison can establish. If needed, model identifying variables first and record that support.
2. Ask one question at a time and wait. Show the constructed contribution after the initial judgment; let the educator accept, check, revise, or refuse with reasons. Provide assistance when requested and record it.
3. Use matched tiles both initially at 30°C: after the same interval, the shaded tile is 31°C and the sunlit tile is 37°C. Ask what this better-controlled, single trial supports and what still needs repetition. Do not carry the original 8°C difference into this new case.
4. Save the initial explanation, support supplied, accept/check/revise decision, explanation of the changed comparison, and the teacher’s next instructional step.

## Reference matrix

These are proposed practices for this setting. Choose by learning objective and task readiness. The six phases are a design lens, not a validated developmental sequence; later phases are not automatically better.

| Practice | Learner | Educator | Institution |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Ask | Ask about the teacher-supplied observations; distinguish asking from acting. | Use a shared screen or prepared contribution and model how to question it. | Set age-appropriate access and data boundaries; no individual AI account is assumed. |
| Understand | Identify changed variables and explain a temperature difference. | Teach or model the missing concept before critique. | Provide accessible materials and ordinary learning supports. |
| Produce | Make a short claim from the observations and show its evidence. | Supply the task frame; keep the taught inference with the learner. | Protect time for learning and define permitted assistance. |
| Judge | Explain why the first comparison is confounded and reconsider matched tiles. | Accept a sound contribution when warranted; use a short changed case. | Support subject-aligned evidence of learning, not automatic AI scores. |
| Codify | With guidance, record a simple fair-comparison check. | Decide whether making a reusable method serves this lesson. | Share teacher-reviewed examples with support and context recorded. |
| Supervise | Optionally inspect a teacher-led two-step example; independent supervision is not required. | Retain control of tool actions and stop the activity when prerequisites are missing. | Adults retain permissions, safeguarding, and institutional decisions. |

## Evidence needed before broader use

Teachers first rehearse with constructed responses, then test the activity only in an appropriate classroom setting. Check prerequisite understanding, access to explanation, and whether the student can explain a new comparison with the recorded support. Track preparation and review time. Do not claim independent mastery from a supported response.

Record the setting, subject, task, participants’ roles, support, observed evidence, reviewer disagreement, and limits. Evidence from one audience does not validate another. A software check or an educator rehearsal does not establish student learning.
