# Method Card: Build a Guided Path for a Faculty Problem

A guided path turns one faculty member's concrete problem into a short, send-able document: the exact prompts to paste, in order, over the workbench bundle. It is how the workbench meets a colleague where they already are.

## Task Brief

- Goal of the task: convert a named faculty problem into a one-or-two-page guided path a colleague can run alone, with a test-before-trust step built in.
- Who the output is for: one faculty member, by name, with their real constraints.
- Output format and length: an email-able note plus one or two pages of steps with exact paste-able prompts; a sample of the expected output attached.
- What the human must supply each run: the faculty member's problem in their own words, their tool access and network constraints, and what they fear getting wrong.

## When To Use — And Not

- Use when: a faculty member has named a recurring, concrete problem ("the reading volume when students submit essays") and has the standing to run a half-day experiment.
- Do not use when: the request is a general introduction to the workbench (send the diagnostic instead), or the problem is still vague (clarify first; the path inherits every ambiguity you skip).
- Simpler alternative that sometimes suffices: the phase placement diagnostic, run with an assistant.

## Steps

1. Clarify the problem: the shape of the work stream, what tools they have, what "getting it wrong" means to them, and what their network can reach. Ask before designing.
2. Place the problem: run the placement logic from the phase placement diagnostic on the faculty member's own task (its question 6 allows this). Identify the earliest phase not yet solid.
3. Route templates: pick the two or three templates the path will walk, in order. Adapt by prompt wording, not by editing templates.
4. Draft the path: exact prompts, one obvious action per step, every artifact term defined at first use, routes named ("the attachment route"), time estimates hedged or absent. State the risks on the page.
5. Smoke test the assistant side end to end with invented material containing planted flaws. Expect it to surface real design gaps, not just prompt bugs. Test the file-attachment route, and require a proof-of-read check (count the bundle's sections) rather than trusting "I read it."
6. Design the pilot: tune on two or three items of already-judged work with the old judgments in view, freeze the wording, test cold on held-back items, and score against the faculty member's own historical judgment by hand.
7. Send it as an idea sized to their time, with the sample output attached, and say plainly what a failed result is worth.
8. Harvest: scorecard, after-action note with matrix check, and any transcripts they will share. Revise the path and this card.

## Review Criteria

- Every step is one obvious action a busy non-technical colleague could take.
- No effort or time numbers the work cannot back.
- The faculty member's judgment decides every consequential output; the path says so where it matters.
- Risks and stop conditions appear on the page, not in a side conversation.

## Stop Rules

- Smoke test fails on mechanics: fix the path before anyone sees it.
- Pilot calibration fails on the tuning items: stop, record what was missed, and treat that record as the deliverable.

## Examples

- One good output: an essay-review path — review standard written once, kit packaged, tune-freeze-test pilot on previously graded essays, supervised first reads with hand-read rules.
- One bad output: untested prompts sent cold, with promised minutes-per-essay savings no one measured.

## Human Review Points

- The owner reads every claim and every prompt before it is sent.
- The receiving faculty member owns every judgment their materials produce.
